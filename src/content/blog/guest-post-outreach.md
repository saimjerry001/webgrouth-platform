**Guest post outreach is the process of approaching a relevant publication with a useful article contribution.** Start by checking its submission rules, then explain what you want to write, who it helps, and why you can cover the subject well. Follow the editor's process through to an agreed brief and publication.

The first email has a limited job: give the editor enough information to assess the idea. A clear reader problem, a specific angle, and a relevant writing sample are more useful than a long introduction to your business.

This guide walks through that process, including original email examples you can adapt to the publication and your actual experience.

## Check how the publication accepts contributions

Open the publication's contributor guidelines before drafting an email. Establish whether it currently accepts outside work and what it wants at the first stage: an idea, an outline, a completed article, or an application form.

These routes differ even among well-known marketing publications. Content Marketing Institute says it reviews completed articles rather than pitches. MarketingProfs requests article submissions as Word attachments and specifies what to put in the email subject line. Check their current [CMI contributor FAQs](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/writing-guideline-faqs) and [MarketingProfs submission guidelines](https://www.marketingprofs.com/write-for-us) before contacting either publication.

Record the submission route, accepted subjects, required format, and any stated response window. Use the contact listed for contributions. A general customer support address may have no role in editorial decisions.

If the site says submissions are closed, move it out of the active outreach list. If its instructions are unclear, a short process question may be more appropriate than sending a complete draft.

Also distinguish editorial submissions from paid advertising enquiries. A publication may offer both, with different contacts, review criteria, and terms. Ask about the route you actually intend to use.

## Write a short research note for each publisher

Read several recent articles and inspect coverage related to your proposed subject. You need enough context to explain how your contribution would fit.

For each prospect, record three things:

| Research note | What to write down |
| --- | --- |
| Reader | Who the publication helps and what those readers are trying to do |
| Existing coverage | One relevant article and the question it already answers |
| Your contribution | A useful next question, example, or method you could add |

For an illustrative agency-management publication, your note might read: “Readers run small service teams. The existing guide explains the sales-to-delivery handoff. I can propose a first-week client checklist covering responsibilities, missing information, and approval points.”

That note gives you a reason to contact this particular editor. It also helps you avoid proposing an article the publication has already covered in detail.

Review the publication itself before spending time on a pitch. Our [guide to vetting guest post sites](/blog/how-to-vet-guest-post-sites) covers audience fit, editorial quality, traffic evidence, and publishing terms.

## Turn a broad topic into a useful article idea

A topic names the subject. An angle tells the editor what the reader will learn from your treatment of it.

These examples show how to make that distinction:

| Broad topic | More specific article angle |
| --- | --- |
| Client onboarding | A first-week handoff checklist for a three-person agency |
| Content planning | How to turn recurring customer questions into a monthly article brief |
| Guest posting | What to confirm with a publisher before commissioning a draft |

Choose an angle you can support with knowledge, research, or a practical example. If you plan to include client information, obtain permission and remove confidential details. If an example is hypothetical, label it clearly in the article.

Sketch the main sections before pitching. Can you explain what each one will contribute? A headline that sounds specific can still lead to a vague article if you have no useful material beneath it.

Check the destination you hope to reference, too. It should help a reader at a natural point in the proposed article. If you only need to suggest a resource for an existing passage, compare [guest posting and link insertion](/blog/guest-posting-vs-link-insertion) before requesting a whole new post.

## Build a pitch the editor can assess

Where the publication accepts ideas, a practical pitch contains five elements:

1. **A clear subject:** identify the proposed topic without implying an existing conversation.
2. **A reason for the fit:** refer to something relevant you actually read.
3. **The contribution:** state the angle and the reader's useful takeaway.
4. **Evidence you can deliver:** share accurate experience or a relevant sample.
5. **A small next step:** ask whether the editor would like an outline or draft.

There is no universal word count for the email. Keep the information easy to scan and follow any length limit in the submission rules. Avoid a long company introduction before the article idea.

Here is an adaptable example. Replace every bracketed field with a true detail, and change the proposed topic if it does not fit the publication.

> **Subject: Article idea: a first-week client handoff checklist**
>
> Hi [editor's name],
>
> Your article on [specific subject] explains [a detail you actually read]. I would like to contribute a practical follow-up for [the publication's readers].
>
> The proposed article would show how a small agency can organize its first client handoff: who owns each task, what information is needed, and where approvals are recorded. It would include a sample checklist readers can adapt.
>
> My relevant background is [accurate experience]. Here is a sample of my writing: [one relevant sample URL].
>
> Would you like a short outline for review?
>
> Best,
> [your name and role]

Personalization should explain the fit. You do not need to claim that you have followed the publication for years or praise an article you have not read.

If you are writing on behalf of a company or client, identify that relationship accurately. An editor should understand who is proposing the work and whose interests the contribution involves.

## Use a submission note when a complete draft is required

For a publication that requests finished articles, prepare the draft in its required format. Check the headline, sources, author bio, attachments, and any requested declarations before sending it.

The accompanying message can be brief:

> **Subject: [the publication's required subject format]**
>
> Hi [editor's name],
>
> Please find my submission, “[article title],” in the requested format. It explains [specific reader problem] and includes [the useful example, method, or resource].
>
> I have included [the items requested in the guidelines]. My connection to the subject or any companies mentioned is [accurate disclosure, where relevant].
>
> Thank you for reviewing it.
>
> [your name]

Open the exact attachment or document you are about to send. Make sure it is the current version, its links work, and the editor can access it. Do not promise exclusivity if you have offered the same piece elsewhere; follow the publication's rules on simultaneous submissions and prior publication.

## Handle replies without losing the brief

An interested reply is the start of the editorial process. Read the entire response and update the brief before replying.

| Editor's response | Useful next action |
| --- | --- |
| Requests an outline | Send the requested structure and explain the reader takeaway for each section |
| Likes the subject but changes the angle | Confirm the revised scope before writing |
| Requests a full draft | Agree on format, length, deadline, sources, and revision expectations |
| Quotes a placement fee | Clarify the complete commercial scope, content requirements, and link treatment |
| Declines the contribution | Record the decision and close that pitch |

For paid opportunities, establish whether the quote includes writing, editing, images, or promotion. Clarify the payment point and what happens if the article is declined or the proposed terms change. An initial price and a complete publishing agreement are different stages of the conversation.

Keep the latest agreed version of the brief in one place. For teams handling several inboxes, assign one person to each publication so the editor does not receive overlapping messages from colleagues.

## Follow up respectfully

Use the publication's stated review window. If it says reviews take two weeks, allow that period to pass. If it explicitly asks contributors not to follow up, respect that instruction.

When no timing is stated, one short follow-up after roughly a week can be a reasonable working policy. This is a suggested workflow, not a proven universal schedule or a guarantee of replies.

> Hi [editor's name],
>
> I am checking once on my proposal for “[article idea],” sent on [date]. The planned takeaway is [one useful result for readers].
>
> If the subject is not a fit for your current plans, I will close this pitch. Thank you for considering it.
>
> [your name]

Reply in the existing thread and keep the follow-up focused on the same proposal. If you receive a refusal or a request to stop, close the thread. After an unanswered follow-up, move on unless the editor has invited another contact.

## Agree on links before writing the final draft

Ask what references are allowed in the article and author bio. Share the proposed destination and explain how it supports the reader. The editor should be free to request a better source, change an anchor, or remove an unnecessary reference.

Google's guidance on [large-scale article campaigns](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2017/05/a-reminder-about-links-in-large-scale) distinguishes useful contributions from campaigns built mainly to place links at scale. It flags practices such as repeated content and excessive keyword-rich links. Build each contribution around its publication and audience.

When a placement is paid, confirm the commercial terms and link attributes. Google prefers `rel="sponsored"` for paid links and also accepts `rel="nofollow"`. See its [outbound link guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links). A paid placement should not be presented as an independent, unpaid endorsement.

The agreement should also cover authorship, revisions, expected publication timing, and any correction or replacement terms. Record what the publisher has actually agreed to deliver.

## Track replies through to publication

Use a simple record for each publication: domain, contribution contact, submission rules, proposed topic, date sent, response, owner, next action, and final article URL.

Keep these stages separate:

- **Interested:** the editor wants more information.
- **Brief agreed:** the scope and requirements are clear.
- **Draft under review:** the article has been submitted.
- **Published:** you have checked the live page.

This prevents a positive reply from being reported as a completed placement. It also shows where work is waiting: initial review, drafting, revisions, or the publishing queue.

Review patterns across comparable prospects. Repeated comments about audience fit suggest revisiting publisher selection. Requests for stronger examples suggest improving the proposed contribution. An absence of replies calls for checking the submission route and the pitch before increasing volume.

Once an article is live, inspect its text, byline, references, and destination links. Measure relevant referral visits or enquiries where reporting is available. Use what you learn to improve your next brief and your wider [content strategy](/blog/content-strategy-organic-traffic).

## Common questions

### Should I write the article before contacting an editor?

Follow the publication's guidelines. Some ask for ideas first; others only review completed drafts. When pitches are accepted, agreeing on an outline can reduce the chance of writing an article outside the editor's scope.

### How many ideas should I include in one pitch?

Use the requested number when the guidelines specify one. Otherwise, one clearly developed idea is a useful starting point. Add an alternative only when it offers a distinct, relevant angle you could also deliver.

### Can I pitch without previously published guest posts?

Check the eligibility rules. Where a publication accepts new contributors, you can share a relevant article on your own site or another writing sample it permits. Describe your experience accurately and show how you would approach the subject.

### Does an accepted pitch guarantee publication?

No. The draft may still require review and revisions. Confirm the remaining steps and only mark the contribution as published after checking the live article.
