A website can have a strong authority score and still be the wrong choice for your campaign. If you want customers in the USA or UK, you need to check who the website reaches, what it publishes and whether the proposed article fits.

The same checks apply to every country. This guide uses the USA and UK as practical examples for two high-demand markets.

Use this process before approving any guest post website.

## Start with the target market

First decide which country matters for the target page. A local UK service normally needs readers, topics and search visibility connected to the UK. A US software company may need a wider business or technology audience with strong US traffic.

Ask for the website's main traffic countries. Then review some of its ranking pages to see whether the audience matches the report.

Country fit does not require a country-code domain. A `.com` website can still have a mainly US or UK audience.

## Check topical relevance

Look at the website's main categories and its recent articles. Your proposed topic should feel normal beside them.

Relevance can appear at several levels:

- the whole website covers your industry;
- one category matches your topic;
- the article answers a question your customers ask;
- the paragraph around the link explains why the destination is useful.

A close topic match is usually more helpful than choosing a broad website only for a larger metric.

## Review traffic beyond the headline number

Estimated organic traffic is useful, but one number does not tell the full story. Check whether traffic looks steady, whether ranking topics match the website and whether recent articles receive search visibility.

Be careful when a website has high reported traffic from topics unrelated to its main purpose. Those visitors may have no value for your campaign.

## Look at recent publishing quality

Open several articles published during the last few months. Check whether they are clear, original, properly formatted and useful.

Warning signs include:

- many unrelated sponsored topics published together;
- thin articles created around exact-match anchors;
- broken pages or poor mobile formatting;
- excessive outbound links;
- no clear categories or editorial focus.

Good design alone does not prove quality, but careless publishing often shows how the website treats contributed content.

## Confirm Google indexation

Check whether recent articles can be found in Google. One missing page is not enough to judge a whole website, but a repeated pattern needs an explanation.

Confirm what happens if your article is not indexed within the agreed period. The publisher or service provider should explain the indexation and replacement terms before you order.

## Agree on the complete publishing terms

Before approval, record:

- the final website;
- article word count;
- allowed topic and category;
- link type;
- any sponsored label or attribute;
- maximum links allowed;
- turnaround time;
- permanence or replacement period;
- final price and payment point.

Written terms prevent confusion after the article is submitted.

## Choose the website and topic together

Do not select a domain first and force a topic into it later. Review the publication and plan an article its readers would genuinely choose to read.

The destination link should support the discussion. Use a natural brand or descriptive anchor when it reads better than a repeated commercial keyword.

## Keep a placement record

Save the approved domain, article title, target URL, anchor, live URL, publication date and agreed terms. Review the link after it goes live and check it again during the replacement period.
