**Anchor text is the clickable wording in a text link. Choose words that tell the reader what the destination offers and fit naturally into the surrounding sentence.** Before approving the wording, open the target page and check that it delivers what the link promises.

For example, a link labelled “publisher review checklist” should lead to a useful checklist. If it opens a pricing page instead, changing the destination or the label is more helpful than adding another keyword.

This guide explains the common anchor-text labels, shows practical edits, and gives you a review process for links on your own website and in articles published elsewhere.

## What part of a link is the anchor text?

In this sentence, the linked words are the anchor text:

> Prepare your first message with our [guest post outreach guide](/blog/guest-post-outreach).

The words “guest post outreach guide” describe the resource. The address behind them identifies the destination. In HTML, the same link looks like this:

```html
<a href="/blog/guest-post-outreach">guest post outreach guide</a>
```

The `href` contains the address; the text between the opening and closing tags is what the reader sees. MDN's [HTML link documentation](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/a) explains how this element works.

A text link may appear in a paragraph, menu, card, or other part of a page. When it sits inside the main article and supports the surrounding discussion, it is a [contextual link](/blog/ultimate-guide-contextual-links). Its wording and its location both deserve review.

## Common types of anchor text

Writers and SEO teams use labels to describe how an anchor is worded. These are useful when discussing a draft, but the label alone cannot decide whether the link fits.

| Label | Example wording | What the label describes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Branded | Web Grouth | A company, product, or organization name |
| Exact match | guest posting | Wording that matches a chosen target phrase |
| Partial match | planning your guest posting campaign | A target phrase within longer wording |
| Descriptive | publisher review checklist | The resource or task the destination supports |
| Article title | Guest Post Outreach: How to Pitch Editors | The title of the destination article |
| URL | www.webgrouth.com | The address displayed as the link text |
| Generic | click here | An instruction with little detail about the destination |

These categories can overlap. “Guest post outreach guide” is descriptive and may also include a target phrase. That overlap is fine; use whichever description makes the editorial discussion clearer.

An exact-match phrase can make sense when it accurately names the subject in a normal sentence. Equally, a brand name can be unhelpful if the reader needs a particular worksheet and the link opens a general homepage. Review the actual relationship between the sentence and the target.

## Ask three questions before choosing the wording

### 1. What will the reader find?

Open the destination and identify its main purpose. Is it a tutorial, a service description, a research paper, a downloadable file, or an interactive tool?

Then name the resource in language your audience understands. “Compare publishing options” suits a comparison page. “Download the checklist” suits a downloadable checklist. For a page that only explains how to make one, “guide to creating a checklist” sets a more accurate expectation.

Be careful with claims inside links. Words such as “free,” “complete,” or “latest” create promises. Use them when the destination supports them, and review them when the page changes.

### 2. Why does the link belong in this sentence?

Write the point you want to make, then decide whether the reader would benefit from a reference. A link may supply evidence, define a term, show a worked example, or offer a useful next step.

Consider this illustrative passage:

> Before requesting a placement, record the publication's audience, editorial rules, and proposed section. A publisher review checklist can help you keep those notes consistent.

The phrase “publisher review checklist” has a clear job here. Linking an unrelated service phrase in the same passage would leave the reader with a less useful route.

If you cannot explain why the reference belongs, revisit the destination or remove the link from that passage. You can place it elsewhere when there is a better reason to introduce it.

### 3. Can the linked phrase be easier to read?

Keep enough detail to identify the destination. Remove words that add no useful meaning, and read the complete sentence aloud after editing it.

Google recommends descriptive, reasonably concise wording that fits the linked page and its context. It also advises against cramming keywords into anchors and placing links directly beside one another without useful surrounding text. See [Google's link best practices](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable).

Use those principles as editing questions. A short resource name may be enough in one paragraph, while a research title may need more words in another.

## Choose anchors for the situation

### Internal links on your own website

An internal link takes readers to another page on the same website. You can usually edit both the wording and the destination, so review them together.

For example, an introductory article can point to a detailed process with wording such as “how to review a publisher.” A service page might link to the same guide as “our publisher review process.” Both can be useful if the surrounding copy explains why the reader would want it.

Keep labels consistent when consistency helps navigation. A menu item does not need a new synonym on every page. Within articles, write the sentence for its local context and use the description that fits.

When planning new posts, identify the existing resources that help the reader finish the task. That makes link choices part of the [content strategy](/blog/content-strategy-organic-traffic), rather than a list of phrases added after the draft is finished.

### References in another publisher's article

For a guest post or suggested editorial reference, share the destination and explain what it contributes. You can propose wording, but the publisher should review the sentence and retain editorial control.

A useful brief might say: “This page contains the checklist mentioned in the draft. Please reference it where the checklist is introduced, using wording that fits your house style.”

That gives the editor the information needed to make a decision. If the editor suggests a different phrase, assess whether it still describes the resource accurately.

### Paid placements

The visible anchor and the link's HTML attributes are separate parts of the placement. A clearly written phrase does not disclose or qualify a paid relationship by itself.

Google prefers `rel="sponsored"` for paid links and accepts `rel="nofollow"` as well. Confirm the treatment before approving a commercial placement. [Google's guidance on qualifying outbound links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links) describes these attributes.

Also review the purpose of the campaign. Google's [link spam policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam) covers links created primarily to manipulate rankings, including paid links that pass ranking credit and certain uses of optimized anchors. Changing the visible wording does not resolve an inappropriate arrangement.

## Five practical anchor-text edits

The examples below are editorial suggestions. Choose the wording that matches the actual destination; there is no requirement to copy these phrases.

| Draft wording | Clearer wording | Reason for the edit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| click here | publisher review checklist | Names the resource |
| best cheap guest post SEO link building services | guest posting options | Removes a string of competing promotional phrases |
| download the template | guide to creating a template | Fits a destination that provides instructions rather than a file |
| this entire paragraph about preparing a client handoff | client handoff checklist | Keeps the linked section focused on the relevant resource |
| our research | 2026 customer survey methods | Identifies which research page the reader will open |

The final example assumes that a 2026 survey and its methods page actually exist. Do not add a date or a claim of research merely to make the anchor sound specific.

A useful review order is **destination, sentence, then wording**. If the target is wrong, shortening the anchor will not solve the reader's problem. If the target is right but the sentence changes subject abruptly, revise the passage before polishing the phrase.

## Make link purpose clear for more readers

Meaningful wording also helps people using assistive technology. W3C explains that some tools can present a list of a page's links, making useful link names valuable when readers choose where to go. Its link-purpose criterion allows the purpose to be established by the text alone or together with programmatically determined context. See [W3C's explanation of link purpose](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html).

As a practical editing habit, check whether the linked words remain understandable when you scan them separately from the paragraph. “See the publishing requirements” offers a clearer choice than several identical links labelled “more.” Context still matters, especially in repeated cards and navigation components.

### What if the link is an image?

A linked image needs text that communicates its function. W3C's [functional-image guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/functional/) explains that the text alternative should describe the action or destination. For example, a standalone image that links to a checklist download should identify that download, rather than only describing its colors.

When an image and visible wording form the same link, avoid repeating an identical label unnecessarily. Review the complete link, including its text alternative, rather than treating every image as a separate writing task.

## Review the links after publication

Check the live page as well as the draft. Editors, templates, and redirects can change what a reader encounters.

1. **Open the destination.** Confirm that it loads and still provides the promised resource.
2. **Read the surrounding passage.** Make sure the reference supports the point being made.
3. **Inspect the visible wording.** Look for vague instructions, unnecessary repetition, and claims the destination no longer supports.
4. **Check the complete link.** For image links, include the text alternative; for paid placements, check the agreed attribute and commercial treatment.
5. **Record corrections.** Keep the source URL, current anchor, destination, and requested change together so another person can act on the note.

For a publisher's page, ask for a specific correction with a short reason. For your own site, update the affected link and check the result. A change to a page's content may justify revisiting incoming anchors even when its URL stays the same.

## Common questions

### How many words should anchor text contain?

Use enough words to identify the destination clearly. A short name can work for a familiar resource; a document title may be longer. Edit for meaning and readability instead of a fixed word count.

### Do I need a fixed ratio of branded and exact-match anchors?

Use any proportions you collect as notes for further review. They do not explain why an individual link exists or whether its placement is appropriate. Inspect the sentence, destination, publisher, and relationship before approving a link.

### Should every link to the same page use different wording?

Use wording that fits each location. Consistent navigation labels help readers recognize destinations. In article text, a different description may make sense when the reader is approaching the resource from a different question.

### Can I change an anchor after a guest post is published?

Ask the publisher about its correction process. Explain what is inaccurate or unclear and suggest a suitable revision. The publisher may need to review the change, and any work or charges should follow the agreed terms.
