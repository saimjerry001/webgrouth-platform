**Broken link building works on a simple exchange: you find a dead link on someone else's site, point out the problem, and offer your own relevant page as a working replacement.** It's one of the few link building tactics where you're doing the site owner a genuine favor first, which is exactly why it still gets replies when cold pitches don't.

## Why it still works when generic outreach doesn't

A typical cold link-building email asks a site owner to add something for your benefit. A broken link pitch starts by identifying something already wrong with their site — a dead link hurts their own user experience and, in aggregate, their own SEO. You're not asking for a favor; you're reporting a fixable problem and happening to have a solution.

This changes the psychology of the reply. Site owners who ignore ordinary guest post pitches will often still fix a genuinely broken link, especially on an older, high-traffic resource page they don't actively maintain anymore.

## Finding real broken link opportunities

1. **Start with resource and "best of" pages in your niche** — these accumulate outbound links over years and are rarely re-checked, making them the highest-yield hunting ground.
2. **Use a dead link checker** against a list of relevant resource pages to find actual 404s, not just old-looking links that still resolve.
3. **Confirm the broken link's original context.** Use the Wayback Machine to see what the dead page used to contain — you need to know what it was about before you can credibly offer a replacement.
4. **Only pitch pages where you have a genuinely equivalent or better replacement.** A broken link about "email marketing tools" isn't a good excuse to pitch your unrelated article about social media scheduling.

## Writing the pitch

Keep the email short and lead with the specific problem, not your request:

> **Subject: Broken link on [page title]**
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I was reading your [resource page name] and noticed the link to "[anchor text]" under [section] returns a 404 — it looks like that page was taken down.
>
> I have a similar, up-to-date resource on [your topic] that covers the same ground: [your URL]. Feel free to use it as a replacement if it's useful, no obligation either way.
>
> Thanks for maintaining such a helpful list.
>
> [your name]

Note the "no obligation either way" framing — it keeps the pitch honest about what it is, which tends to increase trust rather than reduce response rates. ## Where broken link building fits in a wider strategy

It's a supplementary tactic, not a primary one — the volume of genuinely relevant broken links in any niche is finite, and it works best as one channel alongside direct guest post outreach. See our [guide to guest post outreach](/blog/guest-post-outreach) for the primary pitching process this technique borrows its structure from.

It also pairs naturally with resource-page link building: many of the same pages you'd target for a broken link pitch are worth checking periodically even when nothing is currently broken, since they update over time.

## Tracking broken link pitches separately

Broken link opportunities tend to have a shorter shelf life than standard guest post pitches — once someone else reports the same broken link, or the site owner does a general cleanup, the opportunity disappears. Log the URL, the broken link, your pitch date, and outcome in your regular [campaign tracker](/tools/guest-post-tracker-template) so you're not re-checking pages you've already pitched.

## Common questions

### Is broken link building considered a manipulative link scheme?

No — offering a genuinely relevant replacement for content that's actually gone is a legitimate practice, distinct from schemes built purely to place links regardless of value. The key qualifier is "genuinely relevant": pitching an unrelated page just because a slot opened up crosses back into spam territory.

### What tools find broken links efficiently?

Standard SEO crawler tools (Screaming Frog, Ahrefs' broken link checker, and similar) can scan a list of URLs for dead outbound links in bulk, which is far faster than checking pages manually.

### How many broken link pitches typically convert?

Reply and success rates vary widely by niche and pitch quality, but broken link pitches often outperform generic cold outreach because the underlying request is smaller and more clearly beneficial to the recipient. Track your own rate rather than assuming a benchmark.

### Should I always offer my own page as the replacement?

Not necessarily — if you don't have a genuinely equivalent resource, a purely informational note about the broken link (with no pitch attached) is still a legitimate, relationship-building outreach move, and can lead to other opportunities later.
