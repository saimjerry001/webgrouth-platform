**To vet a guest post site, check its audience, recent articles, search traffic, publishing history, and link terms. Then inspect the section where your article will appear. A strong domain score alone cannot tell you whether a placement is worth your budget.**

A publisher can look impressive in a spreadsheet and still be a poor fit for your business. Its traffic may come from unrelated topics. Its best articles may sit in a different section. The proposed placement may be difficult for readers to find.

This guide gives you a practical way to review guest posting opportunities and decide which publishers deserve a closer look.

Once a publisher passes your review, compare [guest posting and link insertion](/blog/guest-posting-vs-link-insertion) to decide whether your contribution needs a new article or fits an existing one.

## What should you check before choosing a guest post site?

Start with the evidence you can collect. Use this checklist to keep your review consistent.

| Check | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- |
| Audience fit | Relevant topics, reader needs, language, and target markets |
| Article quality | Several recent articles and one older article |
| Traffic quality | Leading pages, search terms, countries, and trend dates |
| Domain metrics | Current scores, their source, and comparable publishers |
| Publishing history | Older versions of the site and changes in its focus |
| Outbound links | Where recent articles send readers and why |
| Search visibility | Sample article URLs and any available indexing evidence |
| Editorial process | Who reviews submissions and what the guidelines require |
| Placement terms | Agreed section, price, link attributes, and correction policy |
| Final delivery | The live article, working links, and a dated record |

These are review criteria, not a Google certification or a guarantee of rankings.

## 1. Match the publisher to your actual audience

Write down who you want to reach before opening a list of domains. Include the reader's role, problem, language, and relevant market.

For example, a company selling inventory software might want to reach small retail owners. A publication covering store operations could be a sensible fit. A broad entertainment site needs a much clearer reason to reach the same shortlist.

Open the proposed category and ask:

- Does it regularly cover subjects your customers care about?
- Would your proposed article fit beside its existing coverage?
- Does the writing suit your audience's level of knowledge?
- Is there evidence of readers in the markets you serve?

A worldwide publisher may be suitable for a campaign focused on one country. Check the audience evidence instead of making the decision from the domain extension alone.

**Write one sentence explaining the fit.** If that sentence only mentions a high score or a low price, investigate further.

## 2. Read a sample of the publisher's articles

Read several recent posts, including articles in the section offered to you. Add an older post to see whether useful content remains accessible over time.

Choose at least one topic you understand. This makes vague explanations and factual mistakes easier to spot.

Look for specific examples, useful sources, clear authorship, and answers that follow through on the headline. Google's guidance encourages original value, reliable sourcing, and content that helps readers complete their task. [Google's helpful content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

Try this practical test: write down one useful thing you learned from each article. If every answer is a broad statement such as “marketing helps businesses grow,” the publication may offer little depth.

Also read on your phone. Notice whether you can find the article, follow its headings, and use its links without repeatedly dismissing overlays. Record the experience you actually encounter.

## 3. Check where the estimated traffic comes from

Start with the pages and search terms behind the traffic number.

Ahrefs calculates organic traffic estimates from keyword rankings, search volume, and estimated click-through rates. The figure is not a direct count of the publisher's visitors. [Ahrefs' explanation of organic traffic](https://help.ahrefs.com/en/articles/1863206-what-is-organic-traffic-in-ahrefs-and-how-do-we-calculate-it).

For each shortlisted site, review:

- **Leading pages:** Which articles account for most of the estimated traffic?
- **Search terms:** Do those searches relate to your proposed subject?
- **Countries:** Is the relevant audience represented?
- **Trend:** Is performance steady, seasonal, rising, or declining?
- **Section:** Does the category you are buying into have visible readership?

Use a consistent reporting period when comparing publishers. A current monthly estimate and a screenshot from last year do not describe the same situation.

For a larger placement, ask whether the publisher can share recent, relevant reporting with confidential details removed. Keep the date range and traffic source visible. Compare organic search figures with organic search figures.

A sharp decline is a reason to ask questions. A traffic chart by itself does not establish the cause or prove that Google applied a penalty.

## 4. Use DA and DR as supporting information

Ahrefs' Domain Rating measures the relative strength of a domain's backlink profile on a 100-point scale. Ahrefs also advises against judging a website by sitewide authority alone. [Ahrefs' Domain Rating documentation](https://help.ahrefs.com/en/articles/1409408-what-is-domain-rating-dr).

A DA or DR figure does not tell you who will read your article, how carefully it will be edited, or whether anyone will click through to your website.

Compare publishers covering similar subjects. Record which tool supplied each metric and when you checked it. Use those numbers to identify questions worth investigating.

For instance, if a domain has a high score but little visible activity in your target category, inspect its current articles and backlink sources before accepting the price.

**Keep audience fit and placement quality in the decision even when the metrics look strong.**

## 5. Review the site's history and current identity

Look at older versions of the website, when available, and compare them with the current publication. Check its About page, editorial contacts, and older coverage.

A change in ownership or subject does not explain itself. Ask what changed and whether the current publisher has built a coherent publication around its new focus.

Google's expired domain abuse policy addresses domains repurposed primarily to manipulate rankings with content that offers little value. Domain age alone therefore cannot establish the quality of today's publication. [Google's expired domain abuse policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#expired-domain-abuse).

Record the evidence behind any concern. A shared layout or hosting provider is insufficient on its own to label a site a private blog network. Look for a broader pattern, and read our explanation of [PBNs and editorial backlinks](/blog/pbns-vs-editorial-backlinks) if you need more context.

## 6. Inspect outbound links and sponsorship practices

Open links in the articles you sampled. Check whether their destinations support a point, supply evidence, or help readers take a useful next step.

An unrelated commercial link inserted into an awkward sentence deserves a closer look. If this happens across many articles, ask the publisher how it reviews submissions and chooses destinations.

Google identifies buying or selling links for ranking purposes as link spam. That includes paid articles whose links pass ranking credit. An editorial review does not create an exemption for a paid ranking link. [Google's link spam policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam).

For paid placements, Google prefers `rel="sponsored"`; `rel="nofollow"` is also acceptable. Confirm the agreed treatment before ordering. A paid placement can still be evaluated for relevant exposure and referral visits. [Google's guidance on paid link attributes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links).

Our guide to [dofollow, nofollow, sponsored and UGC links](/blog/dofollow-nofollow-sponsored-ugc-links) shows how to inspect the actual markup and keep link type separate from indexing.

For the article itself, choose link wording that clearly describes the destination. Our guide to [contextual links](/blog/ultimate-guide-contextual-links) explains how links can fit naturally within useful content.

## 7. Check the proposed section's search visibility

Inspect recent and older article URLs from the same section where your post would appear. Do not rely solely on the homepage appearing in Google.

A search such as `site:example.com relevant-topic` can help you locate sample pages. However, Google says `site:` results are not exhaustive. A missing result does not conclusively establish that a page is unindexed. [Google's explanation of the site search operator](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/search-operators/all-search-site).

For an important uncertainty, ask the publisher to check the exact URL in its Search Console account and share the relevant result. You cannot inspect another owner's private property without access. Follow our guide to [checking guest post indexing](/blog/check-guest-post-indexing) for the inspection steps and a publisher request template.

Review whether the sample page works, is accessible to crawlers, and has indexable content. Google makes clear that meeting its technical requirements does not guarantee indexing. [Google's Search technical requirements](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/technical).

Ask about any `noindex` instruction or unexpected canonical target. A canonical annotation indicates the publisher's preferred representative URL, but Google makes the final selection. [Google's canonical URL guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls).

An indexing refund or replacement promise is a commercial term. Read how it works and what evidence is required.

## 8. Confirm that an editor will review your submission

Ask who will review the draft and what they need before approving it. Request the submission guidelines and a recent example of an accepted contribution.

Useful questions include:

- Can the editor reject a topic or ask for changes?
- What evidence is required for factual claims?
- How are author details and commercial relationships presented?
- Can you check important factual edits before publication?
- Who handles corrections after the article goes live?

The responses help you understand the working relationship. A quick turnaround may be convenient, but you still need to know what review happens during that time.

Choose a topic that fills a gap in the publisher's coverage. A focused answer to one reader problem is easier to assess than a broad article that tries to cover an entire industry.

## 9. Agree on the complete placement terms

Confirm the details in writing before committing your budget.

| Term | What to clarify |
| --- | --- |
| Publisher and section | The exact domain and category being offered |
| Deliverables | Writing, editing, images, and any distribution included |
| Cost | Full price, currency, extra charges, and payment timing |
| Link treatment | Destination, anchor wording, and agreed attributes |
| Publication | Expected turnaround and any topic approval step |
| Visibility | Whether the article joins the normal category or article feed |
| Corrections | Contact person and process for fixing factual or link errors |
| Removal | Any stated duration, replacement conditions, and exclusions |

Ask what “permanent” means in the agreement. Also ask what happens if the publisher closes, removes a category, or changes the article later.

A clear agreement lets you judge the actual offer. An attractive headline price gives you much less information.

## 10. Verify the live article and keep a record

When the article is published, open the final URL and check the delivered work against your agreement.

Confirm the title, article text, author details, images, destination links, and sponsorship treatment. Check the mobile page and whether readers can find it from the relevant section.

Save the live URL, publication date, agreed terms, and any issue needing correction. Follow up at an interval appropriate to the placement instead of assuming it will remain unchanged.

Where reporting is available, evaluate referral visits, relevant enquiries, and other agreed outcomes. A wider change in your site's rankings cannot, by itself, establish that one guest post caused it.

## How to make the final decision

Use three outcomes so that missing information stays visible:

- **Shortlist:** The audience and subject fit, the sample content is useful, and the commercial terms are clear.
- **Hold:** The opportunity may fit, but an important question remains unanswered.
- **Decline:** There is a clear mismatch or the publisher cannot provide acceptable terms.

Mark missing information as “unknown.” Do not turn an unanswered traffic question into an assumed zero, or an unverified indexing claim into a confirmed pass.

### Example: comparing two publishers

The following is a hypothetical comparison for a business selling retail inventory software. These are invented examples, not audited websites or reported results.

| Review area | Publisher A | Publisher B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domain Rating | 72 | 38 |
| Leading subjects | Celebrity news and entertainment | Retail operations and ecommerce |
| Proposed section | General sponsored content | Store management |
| Audience evidence | None supplied for retail readers | Relevant reporting available for review |
| Editorial process | No review process explained | Named editor and written guidelines |
| Initial decision | Hold while the audience fit is investigated | Shortlist for a full review |

Publisher B has a clearer case for this particular audience. You would still need to verify its claims and confirm the placement terms before proceeding.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good DR for a guest post site?

There is no universal DR cutoff that settles a placement decision. Compare similar publications and assess their audience, articles, and proposed placement. Ahrefs itself describes DR as a relative metric. [Ahrefs' DR explanation](https://help.ahrefs.com/en/articles/1409408-what-is-domain-rating-dr).

### Can a site with low estimated traffic still be worth considering?

Yes, if it reaches a relevant audience through channels you can verify. Ask for evidence appropriate to the proposed placement, such as readership in the relevant section or newsletter reporting if distribution is included. Judge the cost against those benefits.

### Does a “write for us” page mean the site is spam?

The page alone is insufficient evidence. Inspect the publication's actual output, editorial standards, and link practices. Use the same review process you would apply to a publisher that accepts contributions privately.

### Should I reject a paid placement with a sponsored link?

Assess it against your campaign goal. It may offer relevant exposure or referral traffic. Google prefers the sponsored attribute for paid placements, so its presence is consistent with that guidance. [Google's paid link guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links).

### Can a publisher guarantee Google rankings or permanent indexing?

A publisher can agree to deliver an article and offer contractual remedies. It cannot control Google's future decisions. Google explicitly states that indexing is not guaranteed, even when a page meets its technical requirements. [Google's technical requirements](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/technical).

## Choose a publisher you can justify

Before choosing a guest post site, be able to explain who it reaches, what your article will contribute, and which terms you have verified. Keep that evidence alongside the price and metrics.
