Guest posting is easier to justify when you can show what happened after publication. A useful report does not depend on a domain score or a promise of rankings. It connects the placement to relevant visitors, engaged sessions, enquiries and a credible editorial mention.

This guide gives you a repeatable way to measure guest posting results without overstating what one link can do.

## Decide what success means before publishing

A guest post can serve several purposes. Write down the primary purpose before you choose a publisher, because each goal needs a different measure.

| Purpose | Useful primary measure |
| --- | --- |
| Referral traffic | Qualified sessions from the publisher |
| Brand discovery | New users and engaged visits |
| Lead generation | Enquiries or tracked conversions |
| Editorial link | Live, relevant, crawlable placement |
| Content distribution | Reads, clicks and assisted visits |

A small specialist publication may send fewer visitors than a large site but produce better enquiries. Compare placements with the goal they were bought or earned to achieve.

## Create a baseline

Record the following before the article goes live:

- The destination URL and the action you want visitors to take.
- Enquiries, sales or downloads from that page during the previous 28 or 30 days.
- The publication URL, section and expected audience.
- The link text, destination and whether the publisher uses a follow, sponsored or nofollow attribute.
- The publication date and the period you will review.
- Any existing brand-search, referral or organic trend that could affect the result.

This baseline stops you from calling normal seasonal growth a guest-post win. It also makes later comparisons possible when several placements point to the same service page.

## Use clean referral tracking

If you control the destination URL, add UTM parameters:

`https://www.example.com/guide?utm_source=publisher.example&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=guest_post_topic`

Use lowercase names and a consistent naming convention. Keep the source as the publisher, the medium as `referral`, and the campaign as a short topic or placement label. Do not create a different spelling for the same publisher in every report.

In Google Analytics 4, the Traffic acquisition report uses session-scoped source and medium dimensions. That lets you inspect visits that began from the publisher rather than guessing from page views. See Google's documentation on [traffic-source dimensions](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11080067).

Test the final URL yourself. Confirm that redirects preserve the UTM values, the canonical points to the intended page, and the page loads on mobile. If a publisher strips query strings, keep a dated placement log and use referral data as a supporting signal rather than pretending the source is perfectly attributed.

## Define an enquiry

“Traffic” is not the same as business value. Define the events that matter to your organisation, for example:

- a completed contact or quote form;
- a click to email, WhatsApp or telephone;
- a booked consultation;
- a download that leads to a follow-up;
- a purchase or trial signup;
- a qualified reply that names the article or publisher.

Track these as conversions where practical. A simple enquiry rate is:

`enquiry rate = enquiries from placement div engaged sessions from placement 	imes 100`

Keep a second number for qualified enquiries. Ten low-intent form fills should not outrank two conversations with a genuine prospect.

## Read the data in layers

Review results at three levels:

1. **Direct referral.** Sessions whose source is the publisher. Check engaged sessions, landing page, engagement time and conversions.
2. **Assisted discovery.** People who first encountered your brand through the article and returned later through direct, email or another channel. Use your analytics attribution reports and CRM notes where available.
3. **Organic visibility.** Impressions, clicks and queries in Search Console after publication. These are useful observations, not proof that a single guest post caused a ranking change.

SEO has many moving parts: content updates, links from other sites, internal linking, competitors and normal algorithm changes. Report a placement as a possible contributor when the evidence supports that language; do not promise a position or claim causation from timing alone.

## Check the placement itself

A traffic report is incomplete if the article disappears or the link changes. At each review date, check:

| Check | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Page status | The article returns a successful status and is publicly reachable |
| Relevance | The surrounding copy still matches the linked destination |
| Link target | It resolves to the intended canonical URL |
| Attributes | Follow, sponsored or nofollow status, if visible |
| Index signals | Canonical and robots directives, plus search observations |
| Stability | No unexpected edits, redirects or removal |

For a practical inspection workflow, see [How to Check If a Guest Post Is Indexed in Google](/blog/check-guest-post-indexing). A live page is not automatically proof that Google has indexed it.

## Build a simple scorecard

Use one row per placement and keep the measurement window the same:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Publisher and URL | example.org / marketing |
| Live date | 2026-09-14 |
| Destination | /services/technical-seo |
| Sessions | 42 |
| Engaged sessions | 31 |
| Enquiries | 3 |
| Qualified enquiries | 2 |
| Cost | £180 |
| Notes | Relevant UK agency audience |

You can calculate cost per enquiry as:

`cost per enquiry = placement cost div qualified enquiries`

Do not compare this number with a different channel unless the definitions and review windows match. For a new placement with no conversions yet, mark the result as “too early” rather than zero.

## Review at useful intervals

Use a short and a long review:

- **7 days:** confirm the page is live, the link works and tracking is collecting data.
- **28–30 days:** assess referral quality, enquiries and assisted discovery.
- **90 days:** review whether the placement remains live and whether branded or organic activity has changed.

Some publications have a small but steady audience. A slow trickle of relevant visits can be more valuable than a one-day spike. Annotate your analytics timeline with publication dates and other campaigns so later readers understand the context.

## Common reporting mistakes

- Counting every visit as a lead.
- Treating a third-party domain metric as revenue.
- Ignoring the landing page and its call to action.
- Mixing publisher names and losing source consistency.
- Comparing seven days of one placement with ninety days of another.
- Calling correlation proof that rankings were caused by the article.
- Forgetting to check whether the article or link was later edited.

Before you publish a result, ask whether another reasonable explanation exists. If the answer is yes, include that limitation in the report.

## Final measurement checklist

1. Define one primary goal and one supporting goal.
2. Record the baseline and publication details.
3. Add consistent UTM parameters when possible.
4. Track meaningful enquiries, not only sessions.
5. Separate direct referral, assisted discovery and organic observations.
6. Recheck the live page, link and index signals.
7. Review at 7, 30 and 90 days.
8. Report costs, qualified outcomes and limitations together.
